## 2. Your brain has two modes

Your brain works in two very different ways, and good learners switch between them on purpose.

- **Focused mode** — you concentrate hard on one thing. Your thoughts travel along paths you already know, like a pinball bouncing between tightly packed bumpers.
- **Diffuse mode** — your mind relaxes and wanders. Thoughts travel far and make unexpected connections, like a pinball on a board where the bumpers are spread wide apart.

<PinballToggle lang="en" />

<Callout type="key" title="Stuck? Switch modes.">
  If you have been stuck on a problem for a while, stop. Take a walk, take a shower, draw something — then come back. Your brain keeps working on the problem in the background.
</Callout>

One warning: diffuse mode only helps **after** you have focused first. And scrolling your phone is not diffuse mode — your attention is still being pulled somewhere.

<Callout type="story" title="Edison's trick">
  Thomas Edison and the painter Salvador Dalí both liked to doze in a chair while holding something — a ball or a key. When they fell asleep, it dropped, the sound woke them up, and they wrote down the ideas that had just appeared from the diffuse mode.
</Callout>

Every programmer knows this one too: you stare at a bug for an hour, give up, go make a coffee — and the answer arrives on the way back to your desk.

## 3. Familiar is not the same as known

<Illustration name="feels-vs-works" labels={['Feels good', 'Actually works']} />

Re-reading and highlighting **feel** productive, because the page looks familiar. But recognising something when you see it is much easier than recalling it from nothing in an exam. Oakley calls this the **illusion of competence**.

<HabitEcho lang="en" template="Earlier you picked: {habits}. These feel good — but they mostly build familiarity, not memory." />

Remember the 60-second experiment? That gap between "I read it" and "I can write it down" is exactly this illusion.

The fix is **active recall**: close the book, pull the idea out of your own head, *then* check. The struggle to remember is what makes memory stronger.

Ways to practise active recall:

- **Flashcards** — question on the front, answer on the back.
- **Practice tests** — every multiple-choice paper you do for the THPT exam is active recall.
- **Teach someone** — a friend, a sibling, or a teddy bear.
- **Blank-page summary** — after each lesson, write everything you remember on an empty page.

## 4. Build a wall, not a pile of bricks

Without review, we forget most new information within days. Every time you review, the memory lasts longer before it fades. That is **spaced repetition**: review after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks.

<ForgettingCurve lang="en" />

<Illustration name="brick-wall" labels={['Cramming', 'Spaced practice']} caption="Cramming is building a whole wall while the mortar is still wet. Spacing lets each layer dry." />

**Ten minutes a day beats three hours the night before.** Same total time — very different result.

Two more ideas that make spaced practice even stronger:

- **Chunking.** A chunk is a bundle of ideas your brain can use as one piece — like a song you know by heart. You build a chunk by understanding the main idea, practising it until it flows, and seeing where it fits in the bigger picture.
- **Interleaving.** Mix different problem types in one session, so you learn *which* method to use — not just *how* to use it.

## 5. Beat the procrastination zombie

<Illustration name="zombie" caption="The procrastination zombie: a habit that runs on autopilot." />

Here is something surprising: just **thinking** about a task you don't like activates the part of the brain that feels pain. Opening your phone makes that feeling go away — for now. That is why we procrastinate. Oakley calls these automatic habits **zombies**.

The good news: the pain fades about **20 minutes after you start**. Starting is the hardest part.

So don't aim for the **product** ("finish chapter 3"). Aim for the **process** ("study for 25 focused minutes"). That is the **Pomodoro technique**:

1. Put your phone in another room.
2. Set a timer for 25 minutes. Work on one task only.
3. When it rings, reward yourself with a 5-minute break.
4. After four rounds, take a longer break.

## 6. Your brain needs a body

<Illustration name="sleep" />

- **Sleep** is when your brain cleans out waste and strengthens what you learned that day. Staying up all night before a test backfires.
- **Exercise** helps new neurons grow in the part of the brain used for memory. Even a 20-minute walk counts.
